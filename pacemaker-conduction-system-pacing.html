Pacemaker & Conduction System Pacing in Solapur & Pune

A pacemaker treats a heart that beats too slowly. Conduction system pacing places the pacing lead on the heart's own electrical wiring so the heart contracts in a natural, coordinated way.

When is a pacemaker needed?

The heart has its own electrical system. When the natural pacemaker (sinus node) fails — sick sinus syndrome — or the signal is blocked on its way to the pumping chambers — heart block (AV block) — the heart beats too slowly. This can cause:

  • Dizziness or fainting
  • Tiredness and breathlessness
  • Confusion or poor concentration

A pacemaker can also be needed after some heart procedures, including TAVI.

Conventional pacing vs. conduction system pacing

In a conventional pacemaker, the lead sits in the right ventricle and the electrical impulse spreads slowly through the heart muscle. The two sides of the heart then contract out of step. In patients who need pacing most of the time, this can gradually weaken the heart in some cases — called pacing-induced cardiomyopathy.

Conduction system pacing (CSP) places the lead directly on the heart's specialised wiring so the impulse travels the natural, fast route:

  • Left bundle branch area pacing (LBBAP) — the lead is screwed into the septum to reach the left bundle branch. It is now the most widely used form of CSP because of stable, reliable lead measurements.
  • His bundle pacing — the lead is placed on the His bundle, the main trunk of the conduction system.

Who benefits most?

  • Patients with heart block who will need pacing most of the time
  • Patients with a weak heart (reduced ejection fraction) who need a pacemaker
  • Selected heart failure patients as an alternative or addition to CRT
  • Patients undergoing AV node ablation for difficult atrial fibrillation

How the procedure is done

Under local anaesthesia, a small incision is made below the collarbone. The lead is passed through a vein into the heart and positioned on the conduction system, confirmed with ECG and electrical measurements during the procedure. The pacemaker is placed in a pocket under the skin. The procedure usually takes one to two hours.

Recovery and living with a pacemaker

  • Avoid raising the arm on the device side above shoulder level and heavy lifting for about four to six weeks
  • Keep the wound dry until it is checked
  • Regular device check-ups monitor the battery and lead
  • Most modern pacemakers are MRI-conditional — always tell the scanning team you have a device

Frequently asked questions

What is left bundle branch area pacing (LBBAP)?

LBBAP is a pacemaker technique in which the lead is placed deep in the septum to capture the left bundle branch, the heart's own wiring. This produces a more natural, synchronised heartbeat than conventional right ventricular pacing.

Is conduction system pacing safe?

Conduction system pacing is an established technique used worldwide and is included in international guidance for selected patients. Its risks are similar to a conventional pacemaker implantation and are discussed before the procedure.

How long does a pacemaker battery last?

Most pacemaker batteries last several years, commonly around 7 to 12 years, depending on how much pacing is needed. Battery replacement is a short procedure that usually does not require changing the leads.

Can I use a mobile phone with a pacemaker?

Yes. Mobile phones are safe to use. It is advised to keep the phone about 15 cm away from the device, for example by holding it to the ear on the opposite side and not keeping it in a chest pocket over the pacemaker.
