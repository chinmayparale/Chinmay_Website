What is aortic stenosis?

The aortic valve is the outlet door of the heart. With age, calcium deposits can stiffen and narrow it — a condition called aortic stenosis. A valve that was born with two leaflets instead of three (bicuspid valve) or damaged by rheumatic fever can narrow earlier in life.

A narrowed valve forces the heart to work harder with every beat. Common symptoms are:

  • Breathlessness on walking or climbing stairs
  • Chest pain or tightness
  • Dizziness or blackouts, especially on exertion
  • Unusual tiredness and reduced ability to exercise

Medicines can ease symptoms but cannot open a narrowed valve. Once severe aortic stenosis causes symptoms, replacing the valve is the definitive treatment.

What is TAVI?

In TAVI, a new valve made of animal tissue mounted on a metal frame is folded onto a thin tube (catheter). The catheter is guided through an artery — usually in the groin — up to the heart. The new valve is then expanded inside the old one, pushing the diseased leaflets aside, and starts working immediately.

There is no cut to the chest bone and no heart–lung machine, which is why recovery is usually much faster than with open-heart valve surgery.

Who is TAVI for?

  • People with severe, symptomatic aortic stenosis
  • Older patients, and those for whom open-heart surgery carries higher risk because of age, frailty, lung or kidney disease
  • Patients whose earlier surgical tissue valve has worn out — treated with valve-in-valve TAVI

The choice between TAVI and surgery is made by a Heart Team (interventional cardiologist and cardiac surgeon) after looking at your age, valve anatomy, arteries and other health conditions.

Tests before TAVI

  • Echocardiogram to confirm how severe the narrowing is
  • TAVI CT scan to measure the valve size precisely and check the arteries the valve will travel through
  • Coronary angiography to look for blockages in the heart arteries
  • Blood tests and an overall fitness assessment

How the procedure is done

TAVI is done in the cardiac catheterisation lab, usually under local anaesthesia with sedation. Through a small puncture in the groin artery, the valve is positioned using X-ray and ultrasound guidance and deployed while the heart is briefly paced fast. The puncture is closed with a closure device. The procedure typically takes one to two hours.

Recovery after TAVI

Most patients sit up and walk within a day and go home within a few days. Normal day-to-day activities usually resume within one to two weeks. You will need blood-thinning or antiplatelet medicine as advised and a follow-up echocardiogram.

Some patients develop a slow heart rhythm after TAVI and need a permanent pacemaker. Being trained in both structural intervention and cardiac electrophysiology, Dr. Parale can manage this in the same care pathway.

Possible risks

TAVI is a well-established procedure, but like any heart procedure it carries risks — bleeding or injury at the groin artery, stroke, a small leak around the new valve, and the need for a pacemaker. Your individual risks will be discussed in detail before any decision.

Frequently asked questions

Is TAVI open-heart surgery?

No. TAVI is done through a small puncture in the groin artery, usually under local anaesthesia with sedation. The chest is not opened and a heart-lung machine is not needed.

How long does a TAVI valve last?

Studies that have followed patients for up to about ten years show TAVI valves lasting similarly to surgical tissue valves. Long-term data is still growing, so regular echocardiogram check-ups are advised.

What is valve-in-valve TAVI?

Valve-in-valve TAVI places a new transcatheter valve inside a previously implanted surgical tissue valve that has worn out, avoiding a repeat open-heart operation in suitable patients.
